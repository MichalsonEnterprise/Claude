## SECTION 03 — ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

**Eyebrow:** SECTOR 01
**Headline:** AI that owns something.

**What we look for:**
Companies whose advantage survives the next model generation: proprietary data with compounding collection, workflow ownership deep in the customer's operations, or infrastructure that other AI companies must run on. Application-layer companies must show why they aren't a feature of the platform beneath them.

**Why the sector matters:**
AI is repricing labor-intensive work across every industry. The economic surface area is enormous — and so is the volume of undifferentiated entrants, which makes disciplined filtering more valuable here than anywhere else.

**Preferred characteristics:**
- Proprietary or hard-to-assemble data with a widening collection loop
- Distribution embedded in workflows, not bolted onto them
- Unit economics that survive inference-cost reality
- Technical depth evidenced by shipped systems, not paper credentials

**Signals we monitor:**
Retention after novelty decay · gross margin trajectory as usage scales · dependence on a single foundation-model vendor · data rights actually secured in contracts · IP position on core methods

**Risks:**
Platform absorption by model providers · commodity collapse of thin wrappers · training-data licensing exposure · regulatory movement on automated decision-making

## SECTION 04 — FINTECH

**Eyebrow:** SECTOR 02
**Headline:** Infrastructure over interface.

**What we look for:**
Companies rebuilding how money moves, settles, and is accounted for — payments infrastructure, credit rails, treasury systems, compliance automation. We prefer the layer other businesses build on to the app competing for downloads.

**Why the sector matters:**
Financial infrastructure is decades old at its core, and every efficiency gain compounds across the entire economy that runs on it. Regulation raises the barrier to entry — which protects the companies that clear it.

**Preferred characteristics:**
- Revenue tied to transaction flow or seats embedded in operations
- Regulatory posture treated as product, with licenses or partnerships in hand
- Measurable cost or risk reduction versus incumbent rails
- Founders with lived proximity to the workflow they replace

**Signals we monitor:**
Net revenue retention · take-rate durability under competition · counterparty and credit exposure · licensing progress · concentration in a few anchor clients

**Risks:**
Regulatory change repricing entire models · incumbent distribution advantages · fraud and credit cycles · thin-margin commoditization

## SECTION 05 — RWA & TOKENIZATION

**Eyebrow:** SECTOR 03
**Headline:** Real assets, real rails, real compliance.

**What we look for:**
Teams bringing genuine off-chain value on-chain with enforceable legal structure: tokenized funds and instruments, asset registries, settlement and custody infrastructure, and the compliance tooling that makes institutional adoption possible.

**Why the sector matters:**
Tokenization is the first credible re-architecture of how ownership is recorded and transferred since dematerialization. Frameworks like MiCA are converting it from concept to regulated market — and infrastructure built early tends to become the standard.

**Preferred characteristics:**
- Legal enforceability designed in, with counsel-grade structuring
- Institutional counterparties in the loop, not just retail intent
- Custody, settlement, and reporting treated as first-class product
- Realistic liquidity assumptions — tokenized does not mean liquid

**Signals we monitor:**
Assets actually on-chain versus announced · regulatory approvals and registrations · institutional pilot conversion · redemption and settlement performance under stress

**Risks:**
Legal enforceability gaps between token and asset · fragmented regulation across jurisdictions · liquidity mirages · custody failures

## SECTION 06 — WEB3 INFRASTRUCTURE

**Eyebrow:** SECTOR 04
**Headline:** The stack beneath the noise.

**What we look for:**
Picks and shovels with measurable demand: developer tooling, data availability, identity, security, interoperability, and the middleware serious applications require. We examine protocol economics as rigorously as protocol engineering.

**Why the sector matters:**
Whatever cycles do to token prices, the infrastructure layer accumulates: every application era has been built on tooling from the previous one. Durable value in Web3 has consistently pooled in the stack, not the casino.

**Preferred characteristics:**
- Developer adoption with retention, not grant-farmed activity
- Revenue or fee mechanisms tied to real usage
- Security practice appropriate to value secured — audits, monitoring, disclosure
- Credible neutrality where the category demands it

**Signals we monitor:**
Active developer retention · fee revenue versus emissions subsidy · integration depth in shipped applications · audit history and incident response record

**Risks:**
Token-incentive distortion masking absent demand · security catastrophe · protocol-level competition from L1/L2 roadmaps · regulatory reclassification

## SECTION 07 — SAAS

**Eyebrow:** SECTOR 05
**Headline:** Durability you can measure.

**What we look for:**
Software businesses with retention economics that prove the product is load-bearing: vertical depth, workflow ownership, data gravity, and pricing power that survives procurement. AI-era SaaS must show why automation deepens its moat rather than deleting its seat count.

**Why the sector matters:**
SaaS remains the most measurable venture category — the discipline of cohort math keeps narratives honest, and durable software compounds quietly for a decade after the hype cycle moves on.

**Preferred characteristics:**
- Net revenue retention above [PLACEHOLDER: threshold] with clean cohort logic
- A wedge that expands into workflow ownership
- Sales efficiency consistent with the segment served
- Data accumulation that raises switching costs year over year

**Signals we monitor:**
Cohort retention curves · expansion versus churn composition · payback periods · seat exposure to AI substitution · competitive pricing pressure

**Risks:**
AI-driven repricing of per-seat models · category consolidation · founder dependence in early sales motion · plateaued TAM in narrow verticals

## SECTION 08 — DEEPTECH

**Eyebrow:** SECTOR 06
**Headline:** Where the moat is physics.

**What we look for:**
Science-based ventures whose advantage is demonstrable in the lab and defensible at the patent office: advanced materials, photonics, robotics, energy systems, computational biology. Here, IP analysis is not a section of diligence — it is the diligence.

**Why the sector matters:**
DeepTech converts research into industrial capability, and its winners are structurally hard to copy. The same difficulty that deters most capital creates the opportunity for research infrastructure that can actually evaluate the technology.

**Preferred characteristics:**
- Verified technical milestones, independently reproducible where possible
- Clean IP ownership — assignments complete, licenses understood
- A commercialization path with named early adopters
- Founders bridging scientific depth and industrial execution

**Signals we monitor:**
Prior art position via PatentChain analysis · milestone delivery against technical roadmap · pilot-to-purchase conversion · capital intensity versus funding realism

**Risks:**
Scale-up failure between lab and line · IP challenges and freedom-to-operate gaps · timeline risk measured in years · capital dependence in weak funding markets

## SECTION 09 — FUTURE OF FINANCE

**Eyebrow:** SECTOR 07
**Headline:** The convergence trade.

**What we look for:**
Companies building where traditional and tokenized markets merge: regulated digital-asset services, institutional market infrastructure, compliant investment products, and the connective tissue — custody, reporting, risk — that convergence requires.

**Why the sector matters:**
Capital markets are absorbing digital-asset architecture the way they once absorbed electronic trading: unevenly, then completely. The firms supplying regulated infrastructure for that transition inherit decades of flow.

**Preferred characteristics:**
- Regulatory permissions matched to the activity, not aspirational
- Institutional-grade operations: controls, reporting, governance
- Revenue from services and flow rather than balance-sheet risk
- Teams fluent in both market structures, not tourists in either

**Signals we monitor:**
License and registration progress · institutional client onboarding · volumes through regulated channels · policy trajectory in core jurisdictions

**Risks:**
Regulatory reversal · incumbent co-option of the category · compliance cost scaling faster than revenue · trust damage from sector-adjacent failures
